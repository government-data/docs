This page gathers, for each of the 18 charts on a dataset's dashboard, an
example, the formula Quality Data calculates it with, and how to read it.
What to do with each chart is in [Reading the dashboard](../charts/); what
each indicator means, in [From results to indicators](../indicators/).

<Aside type="caution" title="Example data">
  The charts on this page use made-up figures about a customer dataset; they are no organisation's
  data. The rule charts come from the same fifteen made-up rules, and the group charts from the same
  offices, so their figures agree with each other.
</Aside>

Notation: $i$ = record, $e$ = entity, $a$ = field, $r$ = rule, $g$ = group,
$k$ = component of the ICD (data reliability index), $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ = 1
when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. The weights, severities and
thresholds below are fixed in the product today. Some concepts of the
original method (the ICD's K, V and T components, a failure's cause,
outliers as probable errors, an SLA measured against a target, a treatment
by band) are not calculated yet: see [Assumptions and
limits](../assumptions/).

## 1. Where the charts come from

Each chart is a query over a **results model** that every evaluation writes
the same way, for any dataset: customers, orders, products, suppliers.
Under each chart, **Query** says which part of that model feeds it, and
**Needs** what a dataset must have for the chart to appear.

| Part                | One row per                                            | What it holds                                                                    |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cut summary         | Dataset and cut                                        | Overall data quality index (IGCD), records, entities, bands                      |
| Rule results        | Dataset, cut and rule                                  | Field, dimension, severity, applies, failures                                    |
| Field quality       | Dataset, cut and field                                 | Mean ICD, empties, distinct values, commonest shape, update recency              |
| Entity ICD          | Dataset, cut and entity                                | ICD, band, segment                                                               |
| Group figures       | Dataset, cut, grouping field and group                 | Records, records with a serious failure, mean ICD                                |
| Distribution checks | Dataset, cut and variable                              | Each drift variable's percentiles, outliers by group                             |
| Queue findings      | Dataset, cut, queue and the cut its findings opened at | Open and resolved findings, their priority                                       |
| Evaluation log      | Dataset, cut and stage                                 | The stage's time                                                                 |

They hold aggregates only; individual records stay behind the permission to
see personal data. When a dataset is mapped, some of its fields take a
role, and each role turns on the charts that need it:

| Role           | Example in customers | Example in orders       | Turns on                                                                       |
| -------------- | -------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Entity key     | Document             | The customer's document | Distribution of the ICD, band transitions                                      |
| Segment        | Customer type        | Order type              | Data condition by segment (with an entity key)                                 |
| Grouping field | Office, advisor      | Store, product, channel | p′ chart, largest groups, groups that changed most, remediation queues         |
| Update date    | Last update          | Last change date        | Update recency, and the ICD's A component                                      |
| Outlier amount | Total income         | Order amount            | Outliers by group (with a grouping field)                                      |
| Drift variable | Income, age          | Price, delivery days    | PSI by deciles                                                                 |

Charts that need nothing (conformity and IGCD by cut, failures by dimension
and severity, fields with the most failures, fields with the lowest ICD,
profiling, conformity by rule, time per stage) appear for every dataset.
The SFC indicator belongs to an [optional
module](../sector-colombia-finance/).

## 2. What a rule reads and when it applies

Every figure on this page comes from each rule's result on each record,
which is one of three:

- **Does not apply** when the record is outside the rule's population or
  does not meet its `when` condition; when the field it checks is empty,
  unless the rule says `on_empty: fail` (`required` exists to fail on
  empties, so an empty value does fail it); or when a value the check needs
  is missing, such as one of the parts of a `sum`.
- **Fails** when it applies and the check does not hold.
- **Passes** when it applies and the check holds.

A rule **reads** every field it names: the one it checks, those in its
`when` and its population, those it checks for emptiness, and the source
fields of a derived field. If it reads one field, it counts in that
field's $C$ component; if it reads several, in the $S$ of each. For
example, a `required` on the legal representative limited to companies also
reads the customer type, so it counts in the $S$ of both fields and not in
the representative's $C$.

Each rule has a quality **dimension**, set by its pattern (`required` is
completeness; `format`, `length` and `domain`, validity; `range` and
`placeholder`, accuracy; `unique`, uniqueness; `fresh`, timeliness; those
across fields, consistency; an `expression` declares its own), and a
**severity** with its weight $s_r$: blocking 1.0, major 0.6, minor 0.3.
Without a written severity, `required` is blocking, `unique` and `differs`
minor, and the rest major. The six dimensions classify rules: they are not
components of the ICD. A `fresh` rule, for example, counts in its field's
$C$ or $S$ like any other, and is distinct from the $A$ component of
section 7.

Each field has a **priority** with its weight $c_a$: critical 4, high 3,
medium 2, low 1. Until someone sets it, it is medium.

## 3. Conformity of a rule

Which rules are met least?

$$
\text{conformity}_r = 1 - \frac{\text{failures}_r}{\text{applies}_r}
$$

$\text{applies}_r$ counts the records the rule applies to and
$\text{failures}_r$ those that fail it. A rule that applied to no record
has no conformity: it shows "—" in the table and is left out of the
drawing.

<Chart id="rule-conformity" />

**How to read it.** A low conformity means the data is wrong or the rule is
badly framed: review the lowest with their owner before fixing thousands of
records. A rule that applies to few records, like R-31 here, swings a lot
between cuts: read its count before its rate. More in [Reading the
dashboard](../charts/#rules-with-the-lowest-conformity).

## 4. Conformity and IGCD by cut

Is quality improving?

$$
\text{conformity}_{\text{cut}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_r \text{failures}_r}{\sum_r \text{applies}_r}
$$

A cut's conformity sums failures and applies over all its rules; the IGCD
is section 8's. The chart shows the last twelve cuts up to the one chosen.

<Chart id="igcd-trend" />

**How to read it.** Both rising is good. The vertical axis does not start
at zero: check the table before calling something a change. Steady
conformity with a falling IGCD is usually outdated data (the $A$
component), fixed by an update campaign rather than case by case.

## 5. Failures by dimension and severity

Which kind of problem dominates?

$$
\text{failures}_{d,s} = \sum_{r:\ \text{dimension}(r) = d,\ \text{severity}(r) = s} \text{failures}_r
$$

A failure is a record failing a rule: a record that fails three rules
counts three. The table adds how many rules each pair has and how many
records they applied to.

<Chart id="failures-by-dimension" />

**How to read it.** Blocking and major first. Many completeness failures
point at the capture form or the source; many timeliness failures, at an
update campaign.

## 6. Fields with the most failures

Where are the failures?

$$
\text{failures}_a = \sum_{r:\ \text{field}(r) = a} \text{failures}_r
$$

$\text{field}(r)$ is the field the rule checks (its `field`), even if it
reads others in its condition. The table adds how many rules check the
field and how many checks they made.

<Chart id="failures-by-field" />

**How to read it.** A field with many failures and many rules may simply be
the most checked: compare it with conformity by rule before deciding where
to start.

## 7. ICD of a field

Which data is least reliable?

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{a,i} &= 1 - \frac{\sum_{r \in \text{own}(a)} s_r\,\mathbf{1}[\text{fails}_{r,i}]}{\sum_{r \in \text{own}(a),\ \text{applies}} s_r}
  && \text{conformity of the rules that read only } a\\[4pt]
S_{a,i} &= 1 - \frac{\sum_{r \in \text{cross}(a)} s_r\,\mathbf{1}[\text{fails}_{r,i}]}{\sum_{r \in \text{cross}(a),\ \text{applies}} s_r}
  && \text{consistency with other fields}\\[4pt]
A_{a,i} &= 0.5^{\,\max(d_i,\,0)/365}
  && \text{only if } a \text{ expires and the record has a date}\\[6pt]
\text{ICD}_{a,i} &= \frac{\sum_{k} w_k\, x_{k,a,i}}{\sum_{k} w_k},
  \qquad (w_C, w_S, w_A) = (0.40,\ 0.15,\ 0.15)\\[6pt]
\text{ICD}_a &= \operatorname{mean}_{i:\ \text{ICD}_{a,i} \text{ exists}}\ \text{ICD}_{a,i}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\text{own}(a)$ are the rules that read only field $a$ and
$\text{cross}(a)$ those that read $a$ and others (section 2). $d_i$ is the
number of days from the record's update date to the cut. $A$ falls from the
first day: 0.94 at 30 days, 0.71 at 180, 0.50 at a year, 0.25 at two years.
It exists only for fields marked as expiring (contact data, income, an
employer), when the dataset has an update date field, and on the records
that have that date: a record with no date has no $A$, not an $A$ of zero.
A component that does not exist for a record (no rule applies, the field
does not expire, there is no date) leaves the sum and the remaining weights
are renormalised; a field with no component has no ICD on that record.

<Chart id="fields-lowest-icd" />

**How to read it.** Unlike the failure count, the ICD weighs severity and,
for fields that expire, age. A field low here with few failures, like the
example's update date, is usually **old** rather than wrong: it needs
updating, not correcting.

## 8. ICD of the record and the entity, and IGCD

How are the entities spread by their quality?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ICD}_i &= \frac{\sum_a c_a\, \text{ICD}_{a,i}}{\sum_a c_a}
  \qquad \text{over the fields with an ICD on } i\\[6pt]
\text{ICD}_{a,e} &= \begin{cases} \min_{i \in e} \text{ICD}_{a,i} & a \text{ critical}\\ \operatorname{mean}_{i \in e} \text{ICD}_{a,i} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases},
  \qquad \text{ICD}_e = \frac{\sum_a c_a\, \text{ICD}_{a,e}}{\sum_a c_a}\\[6pt]
\text{IGCD} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{e=1}^{N} \text{ICD}_e,
  \qquad \text{bin}_e = \frac{\min\left(\lfloor 20\,\text{ICD}_e \rfloor,\ 19\right)}{20}
\end{aligned}
$$

An entity $e$ is the set of records sharing the **entity key**, normalised:
a document field keeps its digits only, without leading zeros; any other
field, its value without surrounding spaces and in upper case; a key of
several fields joins them. A record whose key, or one of its parts, is
empty belongs to no entity: it is left out of the entity ICD, the IGCD and
the bands. $N$ is the number of entities with an ICD. Without an entity
key, the IGCD is the mean of the records' ICD and there are no bands. The
chart counts entities in 0.05 bins named by where they start; an ICD of
1.00 counts in the last, and bins with no entity are not shown.

<Chart id="icd-distribution" />

**How to read it.** Right of 0.90 is the good band, between the lines the
fair one, left of 0.75 the poor one. A tall pile just left of a line is
cheap to move: a few fixes per entity change its band.

## 9. Data condition by segment

Is one kind of entity worse off than another?

$$
\text{Band}_e = \begin{cases}
  \text{Good} & \text{ICD}_e \ge 0.90\\
  \text{Fair} & 0.75 \le \text{ICD}_e < 0.90\\
  \text{Poor} & \text{ICD}_e < 0.75
\end{cases}
\qquad
\text{segment}_e = \operatorname{mode}_{i \in e}\ \text{segment}_i
$$

An entity's segment is the commonest value of the segment field among its
records; on a tie, the first in alphabetical order. The chart counts each
segment's entities by band, the largest segment first. The band is a
classification: the product triggers no action from it.

<Chart id="bands-by-segment" />

**How to read it.** A segment much redder than the rest usually fails on
fields its records do not bring: a process problem, fixed at the source. A
small segment can look extreme with few entities: read its count in the
table.

## 10. Band transitions

Where did the entities go since the previous cut?

$$
n_{b \to b'} = \#\left\{ e : \text{Band}_e^{\text{previous}} = b,\ \text{Band}_e^{\text{this}} = b' \right\}
$$

Entities are matched between the two cuts by a keyed hash of their
normalised key: the key itself never leaves the evaluation. Only entities
with a band in both cuts count; the first evaluated cut has no transitions.

<Chart id="band-transitions" />

**How to read it.** The diagonal stayed the same; above it (towards good)
improved, below it worsened. A large flow from good to fair without new
errors is usually data that expires (the $A$ component).

## 11. Update recency

How old is the data?

$$
d_i = \text{cut} - \text{update date}_i \ \text{(days)}
\qquad
\text{bands: } d_i < 0,\ 0\text{–}30,\ 31\text{–}90,\ 91\text{–}180,\ 181\text{–}365,\ 366\text{–}730,\ > 730,\ \text{no date}
$$

It counts every record of the cut by the days between its update date and
the cut's date, never today. "No date" are the records with that field
empty. The table shows the $A$ (section 7) each band gives a field that
expires.

<Chart id="recency" />

**How to read it.** Data can be correct and still old, and $A$ falls from
the first day, not after a year. A large share in the bands over a year
calls for an update campaign. Dates after the cut are suspect (a wrong
format, a default date): check how they are captured.

## 12. p′ chart by group

Does this group fail more than usual, or is it noise?

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_g &= \frac{\text{serious}_g}{n_g}, \qquad \bar{p} = \frac{\sum_g \text{serious}_g}{\sum_g n_g}, \qquad
  \sigma_g = \sqrt{\frac{\bar{p}\,(1 - \bar{p})}{n_g}}, \qquad z_g = \frac{p_g - \bar{p}}{\sigma_g}\\[6pt]
\sigma_z &= \frac{\overline{MR}(z)}{1.128}, \qquad
  \text{UCL}_g = \min\left(1,\ \bar{p} + 3\,\sigma_g\,\sigma_z\right), \qquad
  \text{LCL}_g = \max\left(0,\ \bar{p} - 3\,\sigma_g\,\sigma_z\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

$p_g$ is the share of group $g$'s records with **at least one serious
failure**: a blocking or major rule that reads a critical or high field and
fails. With groups of thousands of records, the plain binomial limits
almost vanish and every group looks out of control; $\sigma_z$, the real
spread between groups measured with the moving range of $z$ over the groups
ordered by size (Laney's p′ chart), widens them. With a single group,
$\sigma_z = 1$. There is one chart per grouping field, over its 1,000
largest groups; the empty group is left out. There is a signal when $p_g$
falls outside its limits.

<Chart id="p-chart" />

**How to read it.** Only points outside the curves are signals. Above:
something in that group produces more serious failures than in the others,
and its cause is worth finding. Below: something is done better and is
worth copying. Between the curves: normal variation, whatever the ranking
says. If every group rises at once, the cause is in the source, not in the
groups.

## 13. Largest groups

Where are most of the records?

$$
n_g,\qquad \frac{\text{serious}_g}{n_g},\qquad \overline{\text{ICD}}_g = \operatorname{mean}_{i \in g}\ \text{ICD}_i
$$

The fifteen groups with the most records of each grouping field, with their
share with a serious failure (section 12) and their mean ICD (section 8).
Records with the grouping field empty make their own group, "(empty)".

<Chart id="largest-groups" />

**How to read it.** Read it with the p′ chart: a large group with a
slightly worse share moves more records than a small one with a much worse
share. A large "(empty)" is a capture problem in itself.

## 14. Groups that changed most

Which groups moved since the previous cut?

$$
\Delta_g = \frac{\text{serious}_g}{n_g}\bigg|_{\text{this cut}} - \frac{\text{serious}_g}{n_g}\bigg|_{\text{previous cut}}
$$

In percentage points, for the groups with records in both cuts; the
fifteen with the largest $|\Delta_g|$ of each grouping field are shown. A
positive $\Delta_g$ is worse: more records with a serious failure. The
first evaluated cut has nothing to compare with.

<Chart id="groups-changed" />

**How to read it.** It shows where to look, not whom to blame: a group can
worsen because it took records over from another. Small groups move a lot
by chance; check the p′ chart before acting.

## 15. Priority and remediation queues

How much waits in each group's queue, and how much is late?

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Priority}_{i,r} &= \max_{a \in \text{reads}(r)}\ \underbrace{\frac{c_a}{4}\,\left(1 - \text{ICD}_{a,i}\right)}_{\text{business impact}} \times \text{closeness}_a\\[6pt]
\text{overdue}_{i,r} &\iff \text{since}_{i,r} + \text{SLA}_g < \text{cut},
  \qquad \text{SLA}_g = \text{the group's, else the grouping field's, else 30 days}
\end{aligned}
$$

A **finding** is a record $i$ failing a rule $r$; it needs a record key
(the mapping's, or the entity's). Its priority takes the highest term over
the fields the rule reads. $\text{closeness}_a$ is 0.2 except in a dataset
with the SFC indicator, where it depends on how close the field's cell is
to the threshold (see the [optional
module](../sector-colombia-finance/#closeness-to-the-threshold-and-priority)).
Each finding falls in one queue per grouping field: that of the record's
value. $\text{since}$ is the cut the finding opened at, carried from one cut
to the next while the same rule keeps failing on the same record. Per
queue, the chart gives:

- **Open**: the cut's findings; **overdue**, those meeting the condition
  above.
- **Resolved at this cut**: the previous cut's findings this cut no longer
  has.
- **Oldest open**: the days from the oldest $\text{since}$ to the cut.
- **Priority**: the sum over its open findings.

The fifteen queues with the most open findings of each grouping field are
shown. While findings are not yet carried from one cut to the next, every
finding counts from the cut shown, and **Overdue** and **Resolved** show 0.

<Chart id="queues" />

**How to read it.** Red is what to chase first; a high summed priority with
few findings means a few costly ones. A finding is resolved only when a
later cut stops failing it. How the SLA is set is in [Reading the
dashboard](../charts/#remediation-queues).

## 16. Outliers: robust z and MAD

Are there amounts unusual for their group?

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_i &= \log_{10}\left(\text{amount}_i\right), \qquad
\tilde{x}_g = \operatorname{median}_{i \in g}\ x_i, \qquad
\text{MAD}_g = \operatorname{median}_{i \in g} \left| x_i - \tilde{x}_g \right|\\[6pt]
z_i &= \frac{0.6745\,\left(x_i - \tilde{x}_g\right)}{\max\left(\text{MAD}_g,\ 0.10\right)},
  \qquad \text{low} \iff z_i < -3.5, \qquad \text{high} \iff z_i > 3.5
\end{aligned}
$$

For each group of a grouping field, take the typical amount (the
**median**; with an even number of amounts, the mean of the two middle
ones) and how far amounts usually stray from it (the **MAD**, median
absolute deviation). The **robust z** says how many of those typical
distances each amount is away. Median and MAD are used instead of mean and
standard deviation because a few huge amounts would move the mean and hide
precisely the unusual cases; the logarithm makes doubling weigh the same at
any level. Only positive amounts count. Records with the grouping field
empty make the "(empty)" group. The 1,000 groups with the most amounts are
calculated, and the fifteen with the most outliers are shown.

**Example.** With amounts of 1, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 and 50 thousand, the median
is 1.2 thousand and the MAD 0.1 thousand: the extreme value does not
inflate it. Taken as logarithms, the MAD falls below 0.10 and the floor is
used: 50 thousand gets a robust z of about 10.9, an outlier, and the other
four stay within 0.6 of zero.

<Chart id="outliers" />

**How to read it.** An outlier is not an error by itself: the chart does
not tell an error from a legitimate value. Review the groups with the
largest share, fix the errors (extra digits, another unit) and keep the
legitimate ones. In a group with few amounts the median is unstable: read
the count in the table.

## 17. Drift: population stability index (PSI)

Did this cut's population change from the previous one?

$$
\text{PSI} = \sum_{k=1}^{10} \left(a_k - e_k\right) \ln\frac{a_k}{e_k}
\qquad \begin{cases}
  < 0.10 & \text{stable}\\
  0.10 \text{ to } 0.25 & \text{moderate drift}\\
  > 0.25 & \text{significant drift}
\end{cases}
$$

The PSI compares how a drift variable is spread in this cut and in the
**previous evaluated cut**. Each evaluation keeps the variable's
percentiles 0 to 100, exact, with linear interpolation between two values.
The previous cut's deciles are the ten bins; $e_k$ is the previous cut's
share in bin $k$ (about 10 %) and $a_k$ this cut's, both read off their
percentiles, to a resolution of 1 %. An empty bin counts as 0.0001, so the
logarithm exists. There is one chart per drift variable, and none on the
first evaluated cut.

<Chart id="psi" />

**How to read it.** Blue columns like the grey ones is good. If the blue
piles up at one end, the population moved: every record can be valid and
the batch still suspect. With moderate drift, review it before using it;
with significant drift, look for the cause (a new source, a change of unit,
a loading error). Because of the 1 % resolution, a PSI near 0 is
approximate.

## 18. Profiling

Which fields are missing most, and what shape do their values take?

$$
\text{empty}_a = \frac{\#\{i : a_i \text{ empty}\}}{n},
\qquad
\text{commonest shape}_a = \frac{\#\{i : \text{shape}(a_i) = f^{*}_a\}}{\#\{i : a_i \text{ filled}\}}
$$

A value is empty if it is null or only spaces. A value's **shape** turns
each digit into `9` and each letter A to Z, upper or lower case, into `A`;
every other character (spaces, hyphens, `@`, accented letters) stays as it
is, and it is cut at 40 characters. Repeats are not compressed: a ten-digit
mobile number is `9999999999`. $f^{*}_a$ is the commonest shape among the
filled values (on a tie, the first in alphabetical order). The table adds
the distinct values.

<Chart id="profiling" />

**How to read it.** An empty field may be optional: compare it with the
rules. A commonest shape far below 100 % suggests mixed formats, except in
free fields such as e-mail or name, where it is normal; a code that lost its
leading zero shows as a shorter shape. It is profiling: it shows no values,
only shapes.

## 19. Time per stage

Is the evaluation getting slower for the volume it processes?

$$
\text{ms per million}_{\text{stage}} = \frac{t_{\text{stage}}}{V / 10^{6}},
\qquad V = \text{records} \times \text{mapped fields}
$$

$t$ is the stage's time in milliseconds. Expressed per million values, a
larger dataset does not look like a slower evaluation. The table puts the
previous cut's figure beside it; without a volume, the bars are plain
milliseconds.

<Chart id="performance" />

**How to read it.** Compare a stage with its previous value per million,
not in plain time: a larger base takes longer without being slower. Running
the rules usually dominates.

## 20. SFC indicator

Only for a dataset that takes part in the indicator of Colombia's
Superintendencia Financiera (SFC): see the [optional
module](../sector-colombia-finance/).

$$
\text{cell}_a = \mathbf{1}\left[\text{correct}_a \ge \lceil 0.90 \cdot \text{active} \rceil\right],
\qquad
\text{SFC} = \operatorname{mean}_a\ \text{cell}_a
$$

An active record is correct in a field's cell when the field is filled and
no rule that reads it fails.

<Chart id="sfc-cells" />

**How to read it.** Red cells inside the 80–90 % band are where the fewest
fixes turn a cell from 0 to 1; green ones just above 90 % are at risk. More
in [Reading the SFC indicator
chart](../sector-colombia-finance/#reading-the-sfc-indicator-chart).
