This page explains the method behind the product: why quality is measured
with rules on every record, the cycle the work follows and what each
quality dimension means. What each word means is in
[Concepts](../concepts/); how a result becomes an indicator, in [From
results to indicators](../indicators/); the exact calculations, in
[Formulas](../formulas/).

## Why measure this way

Quality Data rests on three ideas from the standard frameworks for data
quality.

- **Quality is measured against written requirements.** ISO 8000, the
  International Organization for Standardization's standard for data
  quality, says data is of good quality when it meets requirements stated
  beforehand. In Quality Data each requirement is a **rule**: a few lines of
  text with a pattern, a severity and the sentence the person who fixes the
  record reads. A figure can always be traced back to the rules behind it,
  and **As read** says each rule in words from the same definition that
  runs it.
- **Every record counts, not a sample.** A rule runs on every record of
  the cut. A rate from a sample hides where the errors are; a rate from
  every record can be split by field, by group and by severity, and each
  failure can go to a queue.
- **A cut can be measured again.** A cut is the data as it was on one
  date, kept in the product's own copy, and dates are compared with the
  cut, never with today. Measuring the same cut again with the same rules
  gives the same figures, so a change in a figure means a change in the
  data or in the rules, never in the day it was measured.

## The quality cycle

The work follows the quality management cycle of DAMA-DMBOK, the data
management body of knowledge published by DAMA International (the Data Management Association): define,
measure, analyse, improve and control.

### Define

The people who know the data write what it must meet, field by field, as
rules. Not every field needs rules: start with the fields whose errors
cost most, and give them a **critical** or **high** priority in the
mapping, because priority weights them in every indicator. A rule counts
only once it is approved on `main`. More in [Write, change and test a
rule](../write-a-rule/) and [Take a change to main](../rules-to-main/).

### Measure

A cut copies the data, and its evaluation runs every rule on `main` on
every record. Each record gets one of three results per rule: it fails,
it passes, or the rule does not apply. More in [Run and schedule
cuts](../run-cuts/).

### Analyse

The dashboard turns the results into indicators and charts: what kind of
problem dominates, in which fields, in which groups, and whether it is
improving. More in [Reading the dashboard](../charts/).

### Improve

Failures go to remediation queues, one per group of a grouping field
(an office, a region, a channel), each with a service level agreement (SLA). Data is fixed where
it lives, in the source system: Quality Data only reads. Where many records
fail for one reason, such as a capture form or a load, fixing the cause
once is worth more than fixing records one by one. More in [Work a
remediation queue](../work-a-queue/).

### Control

The next cut shows whether the fixes held. The trend by cut, the movement
between bands and the p′ chart by group tell a lasting change from noise.
What the cycle teaches goes back to the first step: a rule that fails
almost every record may be badly posed, and a field that keeps failing
may need a stricter rule at the point of capture.

## The quality dimensions

Each rule measures one quality dimension, given by its pattern. The
dimensions come from ISO/IEC 25012, the data quality model of ISO and the
International Electrotechnical Commission, and from DAMA-DMBOK. Quality
Data uses six:

| Dimension    | The data…                               | Patterns                                            | Example on `customers`                                        |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness | is present when it must be              | `required`                                          | Every customer has an e-mail                                  |
| Validity     | has the right form and an allowed value | `format`, `length`, `domain`                        | The e-mail has an `@` and a domain; the region is in its list |
| Consistency  | does not contradict other fields        | `empty`, `sum`, `combination`, `implies`, `differs` | A closed account has a closing date                           |
| Timeliness   | is recent enough                        | `fresh`                                             | The record was updated within 24 months of the cut            |
| Uniqueness   | appears once                            | `unique`                                            | One customer per identity document                            |
| Accuracy     | is believable as a picture of reality   | `range`, `placeholder`                              | A birth date is not in the future; no "N/A" as a name         |

An `expression` declares its own dimension. Two more things a reader may
look for:

- **Referential integrity**, that a code points to something that exists,
  is checked with `domain` against a reference column or a catalog table,
  and counts as validity.
- **Accuracy** in the strict sense, that the value is true, needs an
  external source to compare with. Without one, rules approximate it with
  plausibility: values that are possible but unlikely.

The **Failures by dimension and severity** chart counts failures by these
dimensions. Each pattern's dimension and default severity are in [Rule
patterns](../rule-patterns/).

## Beyond rules

Rules say what is wrong with a record. Three more kinds of evidence look at
the data as a whole, and each has its chart on the dashboard:

1. **Profiling** describes each field before any rule: how often it is
   empty, how many distinct values it has, and its commonest shape. A code
   stored as a number that lost its leading zero shows here first.
2. **Statistical control** asks whether a group fails more than normal
   variation explains. The p′ chart by group draws limits that are wide for
   small groups and narrow for large ones, so only real signals stand out.
3. **Outliers and drift** look at amounts and populations: an amount
   unusual for its group, measured with a robust z based on the median
   absolute deviation (MAD), and a population that moved since the
   previous cut, measured with the population stability index (PSI).

How to read each is in [Reading the dashboard](../charts/); how each is
calculated, in [Formulas](../formulas/). What the method assumes and does
not measure is in [Assumptions and limits](../assumptions/).
